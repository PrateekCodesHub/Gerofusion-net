1. Project Overview

  • System: Multi-Modal Deep Learning System for Geriatric Syndrome Detection
  • Architecture: Hybrid CNN-ANN fusion — combines image analysis with clinical tabular data
  • 5 Syndromes Detected: Frailty, Sarcopenia, Cognitive Impairment, Fall Risk, Depression
  • 4 Input Modalities:
    • Image — 224×224 RGB patient images (face, posture, MRI)
    • Clinical Data — 10 numeric features (age, BP, BMI, MoCA, grip, gait, etc.)
    • Audio — Mel-spectrogram of speech recordings
    • Video — Gait video frames for temporal analysis
  • Full-Stack: FastAPI backend (Python 3.10) + React/TypeScript frontend (Vite)
  • 10 Model Configurations: 5 CNN backbones × 5 ANN variants
  • Best Accuracy: ResNet50 + Deep ANN → 96.1%

11. Explainability — Grad-CAM

Grad-CAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping) — Selvaraju et al. (2017), ICCV

How it works:

  1. Forward pass: input image → get prediction probabilities
  2. Compute gradient of the predicted class score w.r.t. the last convolutional layer's feature maps
  3. Global Average Pool the gradients over spatial dimensions → importance weight per channel
  4. Weighted sum of feature maps × importance weights → raw heatmap
  5. Apply ReLU (only positive influence) and normalize to [0,1]
  6. Resize heatmap to 224×224 and overlay on original image (JET colormap)

Why it matters clinically: Clinicians can verify the model attends to anatomically meaningful regions (facial muscles for frailty, brain regions for cognitive impairment) rather than background artifacts. This is essential for clinical trust and interpretability.

BackboneLast Conv Layer
ResNet50conv5_block3_out
MobileNetV2out_relu
EfficientNetB0/B4top_activation
GeriatricNetgn_pw (pointwise conv)

14. Likely Viva Questions & Answers

Q1: "What is the architecture of your model?"

A: Hybrid CNN-ANN fusion model. The CNN branch processes 224×224 patient images through a backbone (ResNet50 / MobileNetV2 / EfficientNet / GeriatricNet) and outputs a visual feature vector. The ANN branch processes 10 clinical features through dense layers and outputs a clinical feature vector. Both vectors are concatenated and passed through Dense(256) → Dense(128) → Softmax(5) for classification into 5 geriatric syndromes. Optionally, audio (Mel-spectrogram CNN) and video (TimeDistributed CNN + BiGRU) branches are fused too.

Q2: "Why hybrid CNN-ANN? Why not just CNN or just ANN?"

A: Medical diagnosis requires both visual patterns (facial affect, posture, MRI scans) AND clinical measurements (blood pressure, cognitive scores, grip strength). A CNN alone can't process tabular clinical data effectively. An ANN alone can't extract spatial features from images. The hybrid fusion combines both modalities for more accurate diagnosis — mimicking how a clinician uses both physical examination AND lab/test results together.

Q3: "What is GeriatricNet?"

A: A custom-designed CNN with 4 convolutional blocks + a depthwise separable block, specifically designed for geriatric patient images. Block 1 (32 filters) detects edges, Block 2 (64) extracts textures, Block 3 (128) captures semantic patterns like slouched posture, Block 4 (256) learns global context. Unlike ResNet50 which is pre-trained on general ImageNet images, GeriatricNet is trained from scratch on geriatric domain data — making it more interpretable via Grad-CAM.

Q4: "What is transfer learning and where did you use it?"

A: Transfer learning uses a model pre-trained on a large dataset (ImageNet, 1.2M images) and fine-tunes it on our smaller medical dataset. We used it in ResNet50, MobileNetV2, and EfficientNet — freezing the bottom 80% of layers (which learn general features like edges and textures) and fine-tuning the top 20% on our geriatric images. GeriatricNet does NOT use transfer learning — it's trained from scratch.

Q5: "What is the attention mechanism in the Attention ANN?"

A: Soft attention that dynamically weights clinical features per patient. It projects features to a 64-d space, computes sigmoid attention scores (0 to 1 per feature), then element-wise multiplies. This mimics how a clinician focuses on the most abnormal readings — if a patient has very low grip strength, the attention mechanism assigns higher weight to that feature automatically.

Q6: "Why 5 syndromes? Why these specific ones?"

A: These are the 5 most prevalent and inter-related geriatric syndromes identified in geriatric medicine literature. They often co-occur (e.g., frailty and sarcopenia, cognitive impairment and depression) and share risk factors, making multi-class classification clinically relevant. Each maps to an established clinical assessment scale (Fried Frailty Index, EWGSOP2, MoCA, Morse Fall Scale, GDS-15).

Q7: "What is Grad-CAM and why is it important?"

A: Grad-CAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping) produces a heatmap showing which regions of the input image most influenced the model's prediction. It computes gradients of the predicted class score with respect to the last convolutional layer's feature maps, then weights feature maps by gradient importance. This is critical for clinical trust — a clinician can verify the model is looking at relevant anatomy (e.g., facial muscles for frailty, brain regions for cognitive impairment), not background artifacts. Reference: Selvaraju et al. (2017), ICCV.

Q8: "What activation functions did you use and why?"

A: ReLU in hidden layers — computationally efficient, avoids vanishing gradient problem. Softmax in the output layer — produces a probability distribution over 5 classes (all probabilities sum to 1). Sigmoid in the attention mechanism — allows multiple features to be highlighted simultaneously (each feature independently scored 0–1), unlike softmax which creates competition between features.

Q9: "What is batch normalization?"

A: Normalizes each layer's inputs to zero mean and unit variance during training, using mini-batch statistics. Benefits: (1) faster convergence, (2) allows higher learning rates, (3) acts as mild regularization, (4) reduces sensitivity to weight initialization. We use it after every dense and conv layer in our architecture, before the activation function.

Q10: "What is dropout and what values did you use?"

A: Randomly sets a fraction of neurons to zero during training to prevent co-adaptation and overfitting. We used 0.2–0.4 across different layers: higher dropout (0.4) in layers with more parameters (fusion layers, early CNN head) where overfitting risk is higher, lower (0.2) in bottleneck layers that need to preserve information. At inference time, dropout is disabled.

Q11: "Why mixed precision FP16?"

A: Our GTX 1650 has only 4 GB VRAM. Mixed precision uses float16 for forward/backward computation and float32 for critical operations (loss computation, gradient accumulation, master weights). This roughly halves memory usage, nearly doubles throughput, and lets us train with batch size 16 instead of 8 — all without measurable accuracy loss.

Q12: "What is cosine annealing?"

A: A learning rate schedule that follows a cosine curve from initial LR (1×10⁻⁴) to minimum LR (1×10⁻⁶). Combined with linear warmup (5 epochs), it: starts slow → ramps up → gradually decreases following cos(π×progress). High LR in early epochs helps escape local minima; low LR near completion helps fine-tune. Formula: LR = LR_min + 0.5 × (LR_max − LR_min) × (1 + cos(π × progress)).

Q13: "What is label smoothing?"

A: Instead of hard one-hot labels like [0, 0, 1, 0, 0], we use soft labels like [0.02, 0.02, 0.92, 0.02, 0.02]. With smoothing factor ε=0.1, the target distribution becomes: y_smooth = (1−ε)×y_hard + ε/K, where K=5 classes. This prevents the model from becoming overconfident on training data and improves generalization to unseen patients.

Q14: "What is the difference between ResNet50 and MobileNetV2?"

A: ResNet50: 50 layers, 25.6M parameters, uses residual skip connections. Highest accuracy (95.8%) but largest model (98 MB), slowest inference. MobileNetV2: uses depthwise separable convolutions that reduce computation by ~8×, only 3.4M parameters (14 MB), 3× faster inference, slightly lower accuracy (93.2%). Trade-off: accuracy vs. speed/size. Use ResNet50 for best accuracy in a hospital setting; MobileNetV2 for deployment on mobile devices or resource-limited clinics.

Q15: "How does the video branch work?"

A: TimeDistributed applies the same lightweight CNN to each of 16 video frames independently (shared weights ensure temporal consistency). The per-frame features are fed into a Bidirectional GRU that captures temporal dynamics in both forward and backward directions — useful for detecting asymmetric gait patterns (stumble → recover). This is more memory-efficient than 3D CNNs (C3D/I3D) which would need 8–16 GB VRAM, while our GTX 1650 has only 4 GB.

Q16: "What loss function did you use?"

A: Categorical Cross-Entropy with label smoothing (ε=0.1). Cross-entropy measures the difference between the predicted probability distribution and the true label distribution: L = −Σ y_true × log(y_pred). Label smoothing softens the target distribution to prevent overconfidence. This is the standard loss function for multi-class classification problems.

Q17: "What optimizer did you use?"

A: Adam (Adaptive Moment Estimation) with initial learning rate 1×10⁻⁴. Adam combines: (1) momentum — exponential moving average of past gradients (avoids oscillation), and (2) RMSProp — adaptive per-parameter learning rates (handles different scales across CNN and ANN branches). This is well-suited for our hybrid architecture where parameters have very different magnitude ranges between the image and clinical branches.

Q18: "What metrics did you use to evaluate?"

A: Accuracy (overall correctness), Precision (of predicted positives, how many are truly positive), Recall/Sensitivity (of actual positives, how many are detected), F1-Score (harmonic mean of precision and recall), AUC-ROC (area under receiver operating characteristic curve), and Confusion Matrix (per-class breakdown). Per-class metrics are particularly important because syndromes have different clinical consequences — missing a Fall Risk case (false negative) is more dangerous than a false positive.

Q19: "What is data augmentation and why did you use it?"

A: Applying random transformations to training images: horizontal flip, brightness ±15%, contrast 85–115%, hue ±5%. This artificially increases dataset diversity, preventing overfitting to specific image characteristics (e.g., a particular camera, lighting, or background). The model becomes robust to variations in real clinical photos. Augmentation is applied only during training, not during validation or inference.

Q20: "Can this be used in a real clinical setting?"

A: This is an academic research prototype — not for clinical use. For clinical deployment, it would need: (1) FDA/regulatory approval (e.g., CE marking, FDA 510k), (2) prospective clinical validation on diverse patient populations, (3) integration with hospital EHR/EMR systems, (4) extensive bias and fairness testing across demographics (age, gender, ethnicity), (5) formal randomized clinical trials, (6) cybersecurity and privacy compliance (HIPAA/GDPR), (7) clinician training and change management.
